\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:mot}

General statistical inference is a powerful tool for modelling the randomness of our universe, but does not always translate clearly into the modelling of programmatic procedures that dynamically observe, interact with, make decisions based on, and even modify that randomness. Such ``causal programs'' are not well understood by the state of the art.

This report outlines a framework for extending statistical modelling to reason about causal programs with efficient computation and formal specification.

You may skip to sections \ref{sec:found} for a summary of the \AIM semantics used to formalize causal programs, \ref{sec:frame} for the algebraic overview the bridges the gap between syntax of causal programs and creation of ``procedures-to-claim'' proofs that certain procedures suffice to prove certain claims, \ref{sec:cert} for a concretization of how this algebra translates into proof obligations, or to \ref{sec:app} to ground all this high-level wizardry in practical examples.

For the casual reader who wants to understand why this framework is valuable without understanding the framework iteslf, minimally skimming everything up until section \ref{sec:app} may be the most rewarding scheme.

\subsection{Failures of General Inference}

In the modern age, ridiculously high-dimensional ``overparameterized'' inference is broadly considered tractable with two important caveats: \textit{explainability} and \textit{representability}.

First, inference is only answered in an approximate black box sense; given an arbitrarily large set of existing samples, arbitrarily large sets of fresh samples can be generated, but insight into how or why that particular ``fit'' was obtained are not forthcoming. This is often referred to as a failure of \textit{explainability}.

Second, not all problem domains are easily mapped to well-understood statistical models. This can produce a failure of \textit{representability}. In some domains, such as video comprehension, clever ``featurization'' provides this mapping. In others, such as \textit{causal} inference, the mapping relies on subtle, untestable assumptions. The results of inference can be very unstable with respect to these assumptions: small logical errors in the representation of problems can render the resultant analyses completely meaningless.

Unfortunately, in many domains, the parallel goals of explainability and representability are fundamentally opposed. For example, increasing the dimension of parameterization comes at a nearly universal increase in representability at the cost of explainability. Other choices, such as forcing candidate overparameterized models to correspond to simpler ``underparamaterized'' models with human-readable semantics ensures some explainability at the cost of some representability.

\subsection{Partial Solution: Structural Causal Models}

\textit{Structural Causal Models} provide a powerful intersection of explainability and representability in the domain of causal inference. In particular, a \textit{causal structure} is assumed that constrains the dependence of observable quantities of interest to a small set of ``parents''; consisting of a mix of other observable values and hidden/latent ``unobservable'' values. The dependence of each such causal node on its parents is explainable as a small, simply-parameterized model, and the remaining signal that cannot be thus modelled is shunted into raw statistical inference on the unobserved values.

Causal models thus enforce a powerful ``separation of concerns'': the explainable causal dependencies of observable quantities on each other are parameterized seperately from the remaining unexplainable statistical dependencies of those quantities on universal randomness. The parameterization of the model is thus split into the former \textit{causal parameters} and the latter \textit{statistical parameters}.

Thus purely statistical operations (like sampling fresh units, conditioning on observable properties of units, and observing properties of units), and causal operations (``interventions'' that alter the causal mechanisms at play) are equally exprissible in structural causal models.

To this extent, structural causal models can be said to faciliate a nearly-complete reduction of causal inference to general statistical inference.

\subsection{Complete Solution: Specialization to Causal Programs}

Unfortunately, directly reducing the problem of causal inference to the synthesis and use of statistical ``black boxes'' is often unsatisfactory. All the problems of general inference lift through this reduction to become properties of general causal inference: ridiculously high dimensionality, limited explainability, and instability with respect to subtle assumptions. Most crucially, it is nearly impossible to establish that the inferred black boxes adhere to any meaningful \textit{specification} expressed in terms of the causal assumptions.

Our framework relies on simple formal descriptions of procedures for observing and interacting with the world (\AIM programs), and models their behavior directly through an information-processing pipeline. Notable, each step of the pipeline has clearly defined semantics and specifications, yielding composed specifications applying to the results of inference through the pipleine.

Once built, this will allow specifications of strucutral causal models to be combined with specifications of data-gathering programs and query programs to sythensize or validate estimators that directly answer the query in terms of gathered data.

The estimator can be proven sound with respect to all assumptions and specifications with far greater precision that can be obtained directly through the application of general statistical inference.

\section{Foundation: \AIM Semantics for Causal programs}
\label{sec:found}

We define 4 modellings domains:

\begin{description}
\item[$\U$]: domain of ``unobservable states'' - i.e. assignments to the unobservable statistical terms in the structural causal model
\item[$\MU$]: ``causal state'' - i.e. assignments of the particular relationships between observable terms and their causal parents
\item[$\R$]: ``causal program'' - the space of programs that may be modelled
\item[$\D$]: - the domain of ``results''/``return values'' of causal programs
\end{description}

Many design decisions influence the representation of these 4 domains. This report is motivated by a particular presentation of the domain $\R$ of causal programs consisting of \AIM programs: programs consisting of three atomic operations:
\begin{description}
\item[$\A$] : assign experimental conditions, equivalent to statistical conditioning
\item[$\I$] : intervene, equivalent to replacement or removal of causal mechanisms
\item[$\M$] : measure, which produces the observable outputs of the program
\end{description}


Deferring to prior explanations of the exact semantics of \AIM programs, it suffices to note for the sequel that a sufficient description of their semantics takes the form of inhabiting/deciding the relation:
\begin{description}
\item[$\boxed{\MU, U \vdash \R \dto \U}$]: ``program $\R$ produces result $\D$ in the context of $\MU$ and $\U$''
\end{description}

\section{Framework: Information Processing Pipeline}
\label{sec:frame}

Now assume that we have a semantics for causal programs $\R$ that deterministically produce outputs $\D$ in environments consisting of fixed $\U$ and $\MU$:

$$\boxed{\MU, \U \vdash \R \dto \U}$$

Consider a partially Bayesian approach in which a natural prior $p$ for $\U$ exists\footnote{see: max entropy priors}. We can now consider $\R^{\{p}\}$ to be a probabilistic program that produces a \textit{distribution} over outputs $\D$ for any fixed $\MU$:

$$\boxed{\MU \vdash \R^{\{p\}} \dto \P_p[\D]}$$

This can be directly read as ``program $\R^{\{p\}}$ produces results distributed according to $\P_p[\D]$ in the context of $\MU$ when $\U$ is distributed according to $p$''.

The details of efficiently translating representations of $p$ and $\R$ into representations of $\P_p[\D]$ are subtle in the general case, but for small dimension, it is a simple ``counting'' problem: the probability associated with result $\D$ is the sum of all probabilities associated with $\U$ that map under $\MU$ and $\R$ to $\D$.\footnote{in the general case, continuous and/or convex analysis or other approximation like MCMC may suffice}

\subsection{Experimental and Query Programs}

Our key insight for specializing the model domain is to consider a \textit{pair} of causal programs:

\begin{description}
\item[$\Rexp$]: ``experimental program'' - describes the causal program used to gather data about the world; must correspond to a practical procedure that can be performed to build a dataset of outcomes given access to a large pool of individuals or other units to evaluate
\item[$\Rqry$]: ``query program'' - describes the causal program used to define the question about about the world we seek to answer; need not be practically executable itself
\end{description}

In general $\Rqry$ should be small and simple, so that predictions of its outcome are ``human readable'', but this may come at the cost of being practically realizable, but $\Rexp$ may be arbitrarily complex as needed to ensure correspondence to a realistic experimental procedure.

Many interesting properties are representable even in this simplistic domain. For example, if the resultant $\P_p$ distributions are related via a determinstic transform, we say that the query is ``identifiable'' from the experimental data.

\subsection{``Soft'' Posterior Estimators}

We now make a slight alteration to the modelling domain to introduce the idea of ``soft'' estimates of the query program. In the modelling domain represented by figure \ref{fig:two}, distributions on $\D_{qry}$ are not available unless a value of $\MU$ is fixed. Alternatively, in the ``fully Bayesian'' case, if a prior on $\MU$ itself can be fixed, then (joint) distributions of $\D_{exp}$ and $\D_{qry}$ are also fixed. Unfortunately, priors on $\MU$ are not available or semantically meaningful in the general case.

However, if a result $\D_{exp}$ is fixed, then a \textit{posterior} distribution on $\MU$, in which the liklihoods of various $\MU$ are proportional to the relatively liklihood of our fixed $\D_{exp}$ under them, is also fixed. Thus, any observation of the result of $\Rexp$ fixes a posterior distribution on $\D_{qry}$ even without a prior on $\MU$:

Figure \ref{fig:lik} depicts this relationship: fixing $\Rexp$ and $\Rqry$ induces a distribution on $\D_{qry}$ for any fixed observation of $\D_{exp}$. This induced mapping $\alpha$ is called a ``soft'' decision because it does not force choice of a particular value of $\D_{qry}$, but simply weights the relative liklihoods of each potential value of $\D_{qry}$ given the observation of $\D_{exp}$.

The diagram, and in particular the mapping under $\Rexp$ is mutilated accordingly to show that $\Rexp$, in this setting, is evaluated in its ``backwards'' direction rather than ``forwards''.

This posterior-characterized modelling domain already presents specialized opportunities for analysis and inference. In particular, any properties or characterizations of $\alpha^{\{p\}}$ are semantically meaningful properties or characterizations of the ability of the experimental procedure to answer the query. For example, the variance (or entropy) of $\alpha(d)$ averaged across, maximized across, or otherwise weighted across potential outcomes $d$ of $\Rexp$ represents the degree of certainty with which $\Rqry$ can be inferred from $\Rexp$.

For small examples, reduction of the modelling domain to the construction or characterization of this induced $\alpha$ posterior may be sufficient. However, it is still very a high-dimensional mathematical object in the general case, and often further concretization is useful as described next.

\subsection{``Hard'' Estimators and Classification}

Practically, characterizing posteriors on $\D_{qry}$ is often much more information than we need.\\

{\bf
$\beta$ example:
}\\

{\it
For example, if the query program measures an observable value before and after an intervention, we may only care to know whether the difference in those two measurements is at least $\delta$ with probability at least $(1- \epsilon)$ for  some thresholds $\epsilon$ and $\delta$\footnote{for example $\epsilon = 0.05$ is a standard ``p value'' threshold}.
}\\

This may be modelled as a function $\beta$ that maps the complex space of distributions over $\D_{qry}$ to a much smaller (even binary) domain $\mathcal{A}$ representing answers to a particular question about that distribution. We can now attempt, instead of fully characterizing $\alpha$, to just characterize some $\gamma$ that directly provides an answer $\mathcal{A}$ for any observed value of $\D_{exp}$, but still corresponds to a theoretical composition of $\alpha$ and $\beta$. This relationship is depicted in figure \ref{fig:comp}.

Since $\gamma$ seeks to provide a answer that is a concrete value, not a distribution of values, it is referred to as a ``hard'' estimate.

In its simplest form, when $\mathcal{A}$ is, for example, a boolean domain, a complete characterization of $\gamma$ is simply a partition of all possible results of the experimental program into ``yes'' and ``no'' sets corresponding to the ``correct'' (as defined by the remainder of the model) answer $\mathcal{A}$ given that result.

When $\mathcal{A}$ is a largely, but still easily enumerable set, then inference of $\gamma$ is an only slightly more general classification task on potential results of the experimental procedure.

\subsection{$\gamma$-Approximacy}

Notably, the complete modelling domain specified in figure \ref{fig:comp} lends itself immediately to $\gamma$ \textit{-approximate} inference. Instead of directly computing $\mathcal{A}$ via $\gamma$ that is equivalent to a composition of $\alpha$ and $\beta$, we can choose an \textit{approximate} answer domain $\bar{\mathcal{A}}$, related to $\mathcal{A}$ through an \textit{error function} $\mathcal{E}$ (for which $\bar{\mathcal{A}} = \mathcal{A}$ is an obvious and often-appropriate choice with natural choices of $\mathcal{E}$), and obtained via an approximate estimator $\bar{\gamma}$. This new model is depicted in figure \ref{fig:approx}.

\section{Certification Goals}
\label{sec:cert}

The above information processing pipeline is meant to show how ``procedure-to-claim'' proofs decompose into a series of independently-verifiable algebraic transforms. This section does \textit{not} attempt to show how real world choices of $\Rqry$, $\Rexp$, and $\beta$ translate into particular claims inhabited by this proofs; for that, see section \ref{sec:app}.

Given that the user either accepts \textit{claim signatures} $(\Rexp, \Rqry, \beta)$ as given, or has internalized rationale for choosing them herself, the algebraic construction of the estimator $\gamma$ whose semantics define the ultimate claim proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Fix experimental and query programs $\Rexp$ and $\Rqry$, and a prior $p$ on $\U$ (see figure \ref{fig:two})
\item Compute $\alpha^{\{p\}}$ - the ideal ``soft'' decision posterior estimate of $\D_{qry}$ (the theoretical result of $\R^{\{p\}}_{qry}$) given $\D_{exp}$ (the real result of $\R^{\{p\}}_{exp}$) (see figure \ref{fig:lik})
\item Fix a ``hard'' decision $\mathcal{A}$ defined by a mapping $\beta: \mathbb{P}_p[\D_{qry}] \to \mathcal{A}$ - i.e. pick a concrete (non-probabilistic) property of distributions over $\D_{qry}$.
\item Compute $\gamma$ - the ideal ``hard'' decision corresponding to the composition of computed $\alpha$ and chosen $\beta$ (see fig \ref{fig:comp}).
\item (optional): Compute $\bar{\gamma}$ - an approximation of $\gamma$ up to error $\mathcal{E}$ that may be more interpretable or more computationally feasible to evaluate, infer, or represent.
\end{enumerate}

Each step of this operationalization lends itself to formal verification:

\begin{description}
\item[$\boxed{1 \to 2}$] Computing $\alpha^{\{p\}}$ directly (i.e. intensionally via raw distributions) is computationally feasible only for tiny domains. Formal semantics of \AIM programs suffice to form and validate (extensional) algebraic representations of $\alpha$, or verify the sound approximacy of other intensional representations.
\item[$\boxed{2 \to 3}$] Various principles in theoretical statistic justify choices of $\beta$ - for example, maximum liklihood, minimax loss, minimum variance unbiased estimators, information theoretic encoding schemes. Formal verification can show the correspondence between these principled estimators and domain-specific choices.
\item[$\boxed{3 \to 4, (5)}$] Similarly to the $[1 \to 2]$ case, exact specification of $\gamma$ is algebraically complex, and computationally infeasible. Verifiction of algebraic rewrites or computational approximation is highly valuable. In subdomains, \textit{synthesis} of $\gamma$ or $\bar{\gamma}$ up to certain bounded cost constraints may also be enabled.
\end{description}

Together, some or all of these certification goals would provide a meaingful improvement to the state of the art in verified implementation of causal inference.

Since any $\gamma$ compatible with the framework in section \ref{sec:frame} can be thus constructed, any such $\gamma$ can also be thus verified. In this sense, the framework justifies itself as not just a construction of statistical estimators, but a \textit{correctness by construction} doctrine for synthesis of statistical estimators.

\section{Applying the Framework}
\label{sec:app}



The reader's first reaction to section \ref{sec:frame} may center on appreciating from a distance that there's pretty math at play without really understanding what the math means. This section attempts to remedy that.

\subsection{Interpreting Claim Signatures $(\Rexp, \Rqry, \beta)$}

Section \ref{sec:frame} provided the algebraic machinary (fig \ref{fig:comp}) to claim a correspondence between $\Rexp, \Rqry, \beta$, and $\gamma$. We now investigate the consequences and interpretation of this correspondence more directly:

$$\boxed{(\Rexp, \Rqry, \beta) \vdash \gamma}$$

In section \ref{sec:cert}, we briefly called $(\Rexp, \Rqry, \beta)$ tuples ``claim signatures''. Broadly this is because the framework in section \ref{sec:frame} establishes that, given this tuple, there is a natural ``ideal estimator'' $\gamma$ that captures the ``best case scenario'' for attempting to answer a question $\beta$ about the data generated by a theoretical query program $\Rqry$, given only access to the data generated by a practical experimental program $\Rexp$. The interpretations of each element of this tuple are briefly rehashed as follows:

\begin{description}
\item[\boxed{\Rexp}]: A causal program that can produce data drawn from a domain $\Dexp$ given fixed $\MU$ and $U$. Notably, neither $\MU$ nor $\U$ can be \textit{directly} observed through observations of $\Dexp$; the dependencies are subtle, and teasing out programs that identify particular desired properties of $\MU$ and $\U$ is the game. Also of note: to obtain statistical power, the procedure should be run many times.

  Algebraically, each of these runs observes the same ``meta-universal parameter'' $\MU$, as they all investigate the same underlying causal mechanisms in the world, but observes a fresh ``universal parameter'' $\U$ drawn from the assumed (likely least informative) prior $p$. If it weren't for the former consistency, the data generated by each run would not meaningfully build on each other as they would be learning about fundamentally different universes. If it weren't for the latter variation, the data would perfectly predict each other an thus offer no marginal explanatory power.

  Practically, the power of the framework lies in the stability of the proofs and analysis to changes in $\Rexp$ - making ``simulative'' exploration of the design space of possible experiments, and even search/synthesis in this space, natural and efficient.
\item[\boxed{\Rqry}]: Usually, science is communicated both to the public and to other scientists not in the form of raw data but as claims about the world interrogated by that data. One of the key observations of this framework is that the language of claims about the world proved by experiments coincides with the language of experiments used to prove claims.

  For example, the classic ``backdoor criterion'' observes that commonly, the ability to causally intervene on an observable quantity is constrained; one may wish to prove results about a population in which a condition naturally occurs, but may only experiment by artifically inducing the condition; or one may wish to prove results about artificially inducing a condition, but must do so with experiments that only have access to a population in whcih the condition naturally occurs. In both cases, the \textit{experimental program} \Rexp fundamentally differs from the \textit{query program} \Rqry. In the \AIM language, these two programs are $\A\M$ and $\I\M$ programs, respectively, for the former situation above, and $\I\M$ and $\A\M$ programs, respectively, for the latter situation above.

  In the ``backdoor'' case, we ask for sufficient criteria on the causal structure for $\A\M$-$\I\M$ equivalence; i.e. we ask for conditions under which $\Rqry$ and $\Rexp$ may be freely exchanged. This is a simplest possible case, as now $\alpha$ is an identity, $\alpha^{\{p\}}$ is point-mass (dirac delta) embedding, the most trivial Kliesli arrow, and $\gamma \equiv \beta$ for any question $\beta$.

  However, the power of this framework lies in relaxation. In general, $\A\M-\I\M$ equivalence (the ``backdoor criterion'') does not hold, and so the algebraic specification of nontrivial $\alpha$, and thus $\gamma \not\equiv \beta$ is the only mechanism to retain the ability to prove properties of \Rqry given only access to \Rexp.

  In practice, \Rqry may be the first element of the claim signature fixed, as it corresponds to the natural phenomenon we are attempting to model; even if it is not directly observable, and an alternate phenomenon \Rexp must be devised to practically interrogate the world sufficiently to answer the desired question $\beta$ about $\Rqry$. 
  
\item[\boxed{\beta}]: Preliminary, exploratory analysis the data generated by an experimental procedure $\Rexp$ may involve processing and comprehension in its raw form; examining individual data points, reading tables, relabelling rows and columns, computing summary statistics by hand, generating and examining graphical representations of patterns in the data, drawing tentative conclusions based on perceived correlations, etc. However, when the methodology of the experiment is fixed and communicated, the dependence of analysis on the data must be made specific and concrete.

  For example, correlation, expectation, variance, higher order moments, or other statitstics along chosen dimensions of the data may be chosen; and the analysis will proceed not to talk about individual data, but of these derived statistics. These reductions are essential not just for processing experimental data, but for describing the processing of $\Rqry$'s \textit{theoretical} data that we are attempting to model.

  Even in the simplest cases, such as the $\A\M-\I\M$ ``backdoor'' equivalence. We may be attempting only to show that $\mathbb{E}[Y | do(X)] = \mathbb{E}[Y | X]$. In this case, $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are both the expectation operator $\mathbb{E}$ themselves. In other words, $\beta$ is a ``forgetful'' transform that isolates the specific properties of the behavior of $\Rqry$ that we wish to predict. Just as only specific, low-dimensional properties of $\Rexp$'s data will likely be relevant to proving our claim (a forgetfulness modelled by $\gamma$), only specific, low-dimensional properties of $\Rqry$'s data will be relevant to defining our claim; this is $\beta$'s forgetfulness.
\end{description}

Breifly, we now revisit our intepretation of ``claim signatures'':

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  $\boxed{(\Rexp, \Rqry, \beta) \vdash \gamma} \cong (\Rexp \circ \gamma \equiv \Rqry \circ \beta$)
  \caption{definitional semantics of a claim signature}
  \label{fig:clm}
\end{figure}

In other words, the relation $(\Rexp, \Rqry, \beta) \vdash \gamma$ (described earlier through the commutative diagram in figure \ref{fig:comp}) can also be described procedurally as claiming that ``to predict the value of a statistic $\beta$ over theoretical executions of $\Rqry$, it suffices to compute the value of statistic $\gamma$ over practical executions of an experimental procedure $\Rexp$.

As noted above, if $\Rqry \equiv \Rexp$ (the ``identifiable'' case), then $\gamma \equiv \beta$ suffices. However, in general, the power of the framework lies in the specification of $\gamma$, given $\beta$, in cases when $\Rqry \not\equiv \Rexp$.

Noting that many existing problems in experimental design that motivate this framework coincide with $\Rqry \equiv \Rexp$, we now investigate more broadly $(\Rqry, \beta)$ pairs in the absence of fixed $\Rexp$ - namely, we seek to demonstrate the expressivity of this framework for modelling claims without an a priori experimental procedure for establishing them in mind, tentatively referred to as ``subclaim'' signatures.


\subsection{Running Examples: Subclaims $(\Rqry, \beta)$}

For our running example, consider the following observable values of the participants in a study investigating the effect of a treatment on sleep:

\newcommand{\slp}{\textsc{slp}}
\newcommand{\age}{\textsc{age}}
\newcommand{\tmt}{\textsc{tmt}}
\newcommand{\mvt}{\textsc{mvt}}
\newcommand{\flp}{\textsc{flp}}

\begin{description}
\item[\slp]: nonnegative integer - average daily hours of participant's sleep over study period
\item[\age]: nonngeative integer - age in years of participant
\item[\tmt]: nonnegative integer - dose of treatment administered daily over study period
\item[\mvt]: nonnegative integer - index (for example, minutes above resting HR) of daily movement of participant over study period
\item[\flp]: boolean - a coin flip independent of any other observables (used to randomize treatment in RCTs).
\end{description}

Notably, we will not present a specific causal structure over these observables (besides $\flp$, which must be an isolated node); the framework of this report is entirely parametric in this structure, which will become manifest only in the algebraic details that establish or prove $\alpha$, which we take as given.

\subsubsection{Average sleep}
\begin{align*}
  \Rqry &= \M_\slp\\
  \beta &= \mathbb{E}
\end{align*}

As a ``warmup'' - imagine a very simple subclaim: we only seek to estimate the average amount of sleep participants in our population attain. As one should suspect, $(\Rexp, \gamma) = (\Rqry, \beta) = (\M_\slp, \mathbb{E})$ is a natural choice of experimental procedure and procedure-to-claim estimator.

However, it is not the only meaningful choice. For example, accurate measurement of participants' sleep may not be available, and instead, only a proxy for it (e.g. ``self-reported'' sleep) may be available. If only this proxy value may be measured, then $\Rexp = \M_{\textsc{proxy}}$ admits a sound estimator $\gamma$ iff true average sleep is identifiable from average reported sleep.

Alternatively, only a very small population may be available for study, and the behavior of this subclaim can model the extent to which various techniques for attempting to ``boost'' the statistical power of experiments on this small population suceed.

In general, chooseing a very simple subclaim such as this one can provide an extremely meaningful semantics for the evaluation of experimental procedures $\Rexp$ and corresponding estimators $\gamma$ that are hypothesized to correspond to simple measurement subclaims such as $(\M_\slp, \mathbb{E})$.

\subsubsection{Average treated sleep}
\label{sec:ats}

\begin{align*}
  \Rqry &= \I_{\tmt = 1}\M_\slp\\
  \beta &= \mathbb{E}
\end{align*}

This subclaim is intended to address the classical ``backdoor criterion'' $\A\M-\I\M$ equivalence. In particular, this subclaim asks if average sleep of participants given the treatment is identifiable.

As above, $(\Rexp, \gamma) = (\Rqry, \beta)$ may be possible. However, it also may not be. Most simply, we may only have access to observational data about the sleep of subpopulations that do and dont take the treatment of their own accord. Under certain structural causal assumptions, we may be able to add conditioning (i.e. $(\Rexp = \A_x\A_y...\Rqry)$) that renders the interventional effect immediately identifiable from the observational one.

More generally, estimating this subclaim without the ability to intervene or sufficiently condition on the treatment may be as subtle and expansive a problem as the more general case of the prior subclaim.

\subsubsection{Randomized treatment sleep}

\begin{align*}
  \Rqry &= \M_\flp\A_\flp\{\I_{\tmt = 1}\}\M_\slp\\
  \beta(d_0, d_1) &= (\mathbb{P}[d_1 > 8 \mid d_0 = \text{True}] - \mathbb{P}[d_1 > 8 \mid d_0 = \text{False}]) > \delta
\end{align*}

This subclaim now asks a question targetted by the simplest form of the classical ``randomized controlled trial'': if some participants (randomly assigned) are given the treatment, and others are not, is there a measurable differential effect of the treatment in the group that did recieve it?

This verbal phrasing captures the chosen $\Rqry$ above. As always, if $\Rqry$ is directly implementable ($\Rexp = \Rqry$), then such $\Rexp$ may prove most useful for any choice of $\beta$.

But in general, specific choices of $\beta$, such as the ``absolute difference in liklihood of exceeding 8 hours of sleep'' criterion here, can induce correspondence between practical procedures $\Rexp$ that may differ substantially in design from $\Rqry$ - proving the diagram in figure \ref{fig:comp} serves as proof that whatever $(\Rexp, \gamma)$ practical experiment and estimator are chosen faithfully reproduce the results of running the theoretical RCT $(\Rqry, \beta)$.

\subsubsection{Age-controlled variance in treated sleep}
\label{sec:avts}

\begin{align*}
  \Rqry &= \M_\age\I_{\tmt = 1}\M_\slp\\
  \beta(d_0, d_1) &= \frac{\text{Var}[d_1 \mid d_0 > 40]}{\text{Var}[d_1]}
\end{align*}

For a variety of reasons, any of the above subclaims could fail to correspond well to practical procedures and estimators. One could, for example, hypothesize that treatment meaningfully differs in effect for populations over and under the age of 40.

This subclaim targets that hypothesis, by augmenting the experiment from \ref{sec:ats} to measure age before administering the treatment, and then measuring variance with and without restriction to the older population in the gathered data. Notably, there are many different estimators $\beta$ that could model this hypothesis as a subclaim for the same $\Rqry$

This subclaim could be used to directly justify experimental procedures to interrogate the ``age effect'' hypothesis, or it could be used theoretically, to determine whether other subclaims meaingfully depend on/interfere with its results. subclaim-to-subclaim analysis is a rich question further deferred to future research.

\subsubsection{Counterfactual treated sleep - mean improvement}
\label{sec:c1}

\begin{align*}
  \Rqry &= \M_\slp\I_{\tmt = 1}\M_\slp\\
  \beta(d_0, d_1) &= \mathbb{E}[d_1 - d_0] \\
\end{align*}

This subclaim is interesting because it is, in general, decidedly NOT practically implementable; namely, it measures sleep both before and after treatment.

This is a counterfactual the features $d_1$ and $d_0$ of theoretically generated data correspond, respectively, to the actual amount of sleep of the treated participants, and the amount of sleep the same participants would have reported if not treated.

This is a very direct, unambiguous characterization of treatment effect whose only downside is that it cannot be measured in practice. The formal equivalancies to practical $\Rexp$ induced by this framework are thus of massive relevance - as they would enable some of the first verified procedures for estimating counterfactual subclaims such as this

\subsubsection{Counterfactual treated sleep - variance effect}
\label{sec:c2}

\begin{align*}
  \Rqry &= \M_\slp\I_{\tmt = 1}\M_\slp\\
  \beta(d_0, d_1) &= \frac{\text{Var}[d_1]}{\text{Var}[d_0]}
\end{align*}

This subclaim continues on the counterfactual scenario of the above, but instead computes variance ratios as in \ref{sec:avts}. It is included to highlight that there is a rarely a ``natural'' correspondence between $\Rqry$ and $\beta$ - and more realistically a variety of questions/estimates $\beta$ about a given query, or a variety of theoretical data-generating processes $\Rqry$ for a given $\beta$, may all by pertinent and distinctly enlightening.

\subsubsection{Movement-adapative variance effect}

\begin{align*}
  \Rqry &= \M_\slp\A_{\age > 40}\{\I_{\mvt > 60}\}\I_{\tmt = 1}\M_\slp \\
  \beta(d_0, d_1) &= \mathbb{E}[d_1 - d_0]\\
  (\text{or}~ &=\frac{\text{Var}[d_1]}{\text{Var}[d_0]})
\end{align*}

As a final motivating example of a subclaim, we consider this ``adaptive'' design that builds on the counterfactual queries of \ref{sec:c1} and \ref{sec:c2}, and the hypothesis of \ref{sec:avts} to include ``adaptively'' ensuring that older participants engage in significant physical activity over the course of the study.

This particular theoretical query $\Rqry$ would be presented as something like ``our treatment significantly improves sleep across all ages, provided older subjects accompany treatment with exercise''.

Since this subclaim a combination of a counterfactual and adapative design, two features already difficult to reason about in existing experimental design, the ability of our framework to certify its relation to experimental procedures that can be performed or reasoned about with other frameworks, is a significant contribution.


\subsection{Subclaims Review}

Notably, none of these subclaims specified the experimental procedure that could be used to determine them.

In fact, the optimal experimental procedure $\Rexp$ for any of these subclaims may be arbitrarily structurally divergent from $\Rqry$. One may also take any of theses subclaims as fixed, and impose constraints on $\Rexp$, such as limited ability to measure or intervene, known bias present in the experimental population, or any other practical limitations, and simply seek to maximize the faithfulness of the induced $\gamma$ subject to those constraints - often yielding a \textit{very} different experimental procedure from query procedure.

One may even seek to devise a single experimental procedure that simultaneously produces high quality data for multiple of the above subclaims - which lends itself to direct anaylsis of pairs or groups of subclaims via \textit{mutual information}; i.e. for any two subclaims we can attain theoretical bounds on the ability of a single experimental procedure to answer them both.

Exploring the rich structure imposed on experimental procedures by subclaims is a primary research objective.

\section{Conclusion}

Section \ref{sec:mot} motivated the need to specialize the modelling tasks of general statistical/causal inference whenever possible, showing that when we focus on the particular situation of using one causal program to predict another in the context of a structural causal model, the vast majority of the complexity from the general case of overparametric inference can be eliminated.

Section \ref{sec:found} provided sufficient details of the modelling of causal programs to concretize further details of their analysis.

Section \ref{sec:frame} provieded a framework for extending high level mathematical characterizations of causal programs to specifications of concrete ``soft'' and ``hard'' estimators for causal queries at varying levels of abstraction.

Section \ref{sec:cert}  began to discus the application of this framework to certified decision making procedures.

Finally, section \ref{sec:app} walked through a few example applications of the framework.

Together, this report begins to illustrate that the combination of general statistical inference, structural causal models, and concrete semantics for causal programs suffices to specify and certify efficient and explainable causal inferential procedures.
